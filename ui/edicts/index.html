Eight exceptions

An axis toggle is a policy. An edict is an exception to your own policy, and it occupies one of eight slots. The slot is the scarce thing, not the favor — revoking costs 2,048 against 6,144 to issue, because freeing the slot is the entire point of the action.

The rules do most of the interface's work: a dispensation must name a cell whose axis is otherwise forbidden and an interdiction one whose axes are both permitted, so an edict that would change nothing is masked rather than merely wasteful. Click a cell in direction C to issue or revoke.

What operating these settles

Only the slots direction makes the scarcity the object, and the scarcity is the mechanic. Eight is a small number chosen on purpose, and the cost table prices revocation cheaply precisely so that the slot — not the favor — is what a player is managing. A grid shows edicts as decoration on cells and never shows that you have two left; a list of sentences shows what they say and not what they cost. Eight visible holders, most of them empty, is the only version where the budget is a thing you feel before you spend it.

Sentences are the only direction that survives being read aloud. "Perdo is forbidden, except Perdo Mentem" is a rule about a rule, and that is genuinely what an edict is. It is also the only direction that can express the precedence rule — interdiction beats dispensation — as language rather than as a colour a player has to learn. Content declaring both for one cell is rejected at load, so the interface never has to render the conflict; it only has to make the ordering understandable.

On the grid is the most legible and the most misleading. Marks in place answer "is this cell live" instantly, which is the question a player asks most. But it flattens an edict into a cell state, and an edict is not a cell state — it is a scarce, revocable commitment that happens to affect a cell. A player who learns edicts from the grid will be surprised by the budget.

The argument for shipping two of these. The grid answers the frequent question and the slots answer the expensive one, and they do not compete for the same screen space. That is the same conclusion ui/ruleset-symmetry/ reached about pillar #1 — for the ruleset family specifically, "pick one direction" appears to be the wrong frame.
