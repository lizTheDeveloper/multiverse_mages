Choosing who

Six of the sixteen actions carry an entity handle. §4.4 resolves that for an agent — a fixed-length top-k candidate list under a deterministic salience ordering, and the agent picks a slot index. It says nothing about a person, and a flat list cannot express "bless, but only these forty of three thousand" to a human either.

Three directions on the same task. Move the population slider — that is where they separate.

What operating these settles

The population number decides this, and nobody has chosen it. Vision §13 leaves "how many mages does a mature universe hold" to benchmarking. At 48 the roster is the best of the three and the other two look like ceremony. At 3,000 it is unusable and B and C are the only things left standing. Drag the slider and the ranking inverts — which means this is not a taste question and should not be settled by taste.

The finding worth carrying to agent-interface. Direction B shows the player exactly the candidate list the observation already carries. That has a property nothing else here has: the human and the policy network are choosing from the same menu. If k is too small for a person it is also too small for the agent, and if a person needs a different k then contracts §4's "one interface serves scripted bots, Monte Carlo and RL" quietly acquires a fourth consumer with different needs. Pinning k is therefore a joint decision, not an RL one.

Saturation, found by dragging the slider. At three thousand mages every one of the eight slots is a sole-copy holder: a single criterion takes the whole list, and the other 2,992 mages become unreachable by that verb no matter what the player wants. Raising k does not fix this — it admits more of the same kind. A top-k list needs either a blended ordering or stratified slots reserved per criterion, and §4.4 requires neither. This is a live defect in the specified design, not a property of this prototype.

Salience is undefined. §4.4 says the list is "ranked by a deterministic salience ordering" and never says what it is. The ordering used here is invented — mastery at risk, then age, then tier — and it is doing all the work in both B and C. Two different orderings give a player two different games, so this is a content-shaped decision hiding in a data-structure sentence.

Direction C needs something the core does not emit. "Preserve the last copy of a node" is answerable from libraryDependence, which exists. "Who is about to stop being able to teach it" is the mastery-trajectory gap already filed against ui/mage/. C is the direction most aligned with being a god rather than a general, and it is the one that cannot be built today.

Prototype · not the client. The cast is generated from real species.json traits and real node.json names; the salience ordering is invented because §4.4 does not define one.
